Course Purpose & How to Read the Diagrams

Turn familiar chord progressions into a fretboard map. The goal is not to memorize isolated diagrams — it is to see major and minor triads, choose the nearest inversion, hear the top-line melody, and connect those shapes to scales you already partly know.

  • Practice with clean tone and a metronome before adding gain or effects.
  • Say the chord name, inversion, and top note aloud.
  • Begin with the D-G-B string set, then add A-D-G, then G-B-E.
  • Fret numbers on diagrams are absolute. The small number to the left of every box tells you the fret of the top row; a thick top bar is the nut. Open-position shapes show both — a nut bar and a 1 — so every diagram in a row carries the same position reference.
Reading a box: six vertical lines = the six strings (low E on the left, high e on the right). Dots are the notes you fret. A colored dot's label changes with the toggle above — flip to Intervals to see why a shape sounds major or minor, and to Fingers when you're drilling the grip.

The Four Triad Qualities

A triad is three notes: a root, a third, and a fifth. Change the size of the third and the fifth and you change the quality.

QualityFormulaC exampleSound / function
Major1  3  5C E GStable, bright
Minor1 ♭3 5C E♭ GDarker, settled
Diminished1 ♭3 ♭5C E♭ G♭Tense, leading
Augmented1  3 ♯5C E G♯Unsettled, expanding

See the same C root, four ways — watch the 3rd and 5th move (flip the toggle to Intervals to make the formula visible):

Core idea: an inversion does not change the chord name. It changes which chord tone is lowest: root position has 1 in the bass, first inversion has the 3rd in the bass, second inversion has the 5th in the bass.

Module 1 · Reading the Shapes — C major on D-G-B

Three inversions of the same C major triad on strings 4-3-2 (D-G-B). Same three notes (C-E-G), different note on top.

Fingering conventions

  • 1 = index, 2 = middle, 3 = ring, 4 = little finger.
  • Suggested fingering is a starting point — adjust for hand size and the phrase that comes next.
  • Mute unused strings with both hands.

Key drill: G, C, and D in one neighborhood

Correction from the original workbook. The first draft listed the G chord (D-G-B frets 5-4-3) with "top note G." Those frets are G-B-D, so the highest note (B-string, fret 3) is actually D. The top voice of the G–C–D–C loop is therefore D–E–D–E (an upper-neighbor melody), not G-E-D-E. The diagrams below use the corrected top notes.

D4-2-3FirstD

Practice: loop G-C-D-C at 60 bpm — four beats each, then two, then one. Keep the hand close to the strings and listen to the top voice: D-E-D-E.

Articulation menu

  • Short stabs on beats 2 and 4.
  • Arpeggiate low-middle-high, then high-middle-low.
  • Slide into the shape from one fret below.
  • Hammer one chord tone into place while the other two are held.
  • Play only the top two notes once the full shape is clean.

Bass-awareness drill: play the A-D-G triad, then add the low E or open A string only if it belongs to the chord. This trains your ear for when a compact triad works over a bassist or a separate bass note.

Connecting the Inversions Up the Neck

Here is the single most useful "plateau-breaker": the same C major chord lives in three neck zones on one string set. Once you see them as one connected map instead of three isolated grips, you can always grab the nearest voicing.

Drill: play 1st → 2nd → root → 2nd → 1st inversion, moving smoothly up and back down the neck. Name the top note of each shape as you land on it.

Module 4 · Minor Triads & Scale Support

How major becomes minor: keep the root and fifth, and lower only the third by one fret. Flip the toggle to Intervals and watch the 3 become a ♭3.

C minor on A-D-G

Minor chord exercises

  • Major-to-minor switch: C–Cm, G–Gm, D–Dm. Change only the third.
  • Minor inversion ladder: play Am in all three inversions up one string set and back.
  • Relative-pair drill: C–Am, G–Em, F–Dm. Find common tones before moving.
  • Minor i-iv-v: Am–Dm–Em, then i-iv-V: Am–Dm–E (hear the stronger pull of the major V).

Module 5 · Smooth Voice Leading

  1. Choose the nearest inversion, not automatically root position.
  2. Keep common tones on the same string whenever possible.
  3. Move each voice the shortest practical distance.
  4. Practice the top voice separately as a melody.
  5. Release pressure without lifting fingers far from the fretboard.
PassWhat to doTempo
1Place each chord silently; inspect all three notes.No tempo
2Four beats per chord; stop if any note buzzes.50-60 bpm

CAGED Connections

Use CAGED as a landmark, not another memorization project. Form the full CAGED chord, then release every duplicated root or fifth until only three different notes remain — that is your compact triad.

CAGED formCompact triad to locatePractical question
C formA-D-G root-position areaWhere are 1-3-5 inside the full grip?
A formD-G-B second-inversion areaCan I remove the doubled notes?
G formG-B-E root-position areaWhich top note defines the fill?
E formD-G-B root-position areaCan I slide into the compact upper chord?
D formG-B-E second-inversion areaWhere is the nearest melody-on-top voicing?

Final Checkpoints

  • ☐ I can name the four triad qualities.
  • ☐ I can play three major inversions on D-G-B and A-D-G.
  • ☐ I can turn a major triad into minor by lowering the third.
  • ☐ I can play A natural minor and identify A-C-E inside it.
  • ☐ I can choose a nearby inversion instead of jumping to root position.
  • ☐ I can hear and isolate the top voice of a triad progression.
  • ☐ I can place a melody note above a supporting triad.
  • ☐ I can improvise using the current triad tones before adding scale notes.
